The Fourier engine, schematically

The fast Fourier transform's butterfly: split a signal, transform the halves, recombine. The quantum Fourier transform inside Shor's algorithm is the same idea, executed on superpositions — and it is what breaks RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography.

The break

Shor's algorithm breaks RSA and ECC on a cryptographically relevant quantum computer. The timeline is uncertain; the mathematics is not.

The standard

NIST finalized the first PQC standards in August 2024 — ML-KEM, ML-DSA, and SLH-DSA. The replacement algorithms exist. Deployment is the work.

The clock

Harvest now, decrypt later. Encrypted traffic captured today can be stored and decrypted once quantum hardware arrives. Data with a long secrecy lifetime is already exposed.

A migration is a change of authority.

Swapping algorithms is the easy part. The hard part is governing the crossing: what gets re-keyed, in what order, under whose policy, with what proof — across vaults, codebases, protocols, and partners that were never inventoried together.

Every credential, certificate, and trust root gets re-issued under a new regime.
The risk isn't just the new math. It's an unmanaged transition: half-migrated systems, orphaned keys, and nobody able to prove what is protected and what is not.

What breaks, precisely

Public-key cryptography as deployed today — RSA for key exchange and signatures, elliptic-curve cryptography for signatures and agreement — rests on factoring and discrete logarithms. Shor's algorithm solves both in polynomial time on a large enough quantum computer. Symmetric cryptography and hashing are far less exposed; the migration centers on public-key infrastructure.

What replaces it

Lattice-based cryptography, standardized by NIST: ML-KEM for key establishment, ML-DSA for signatures, SLH-DSA as a hash-based signature backup. Migration in practice is hybrid first — classical and post-quantum algorithms combined — so no system is left unprotected during the transition.

The platform

One orchestration layer for the whole migration, from discovery to audit evidence.

01

Discover & audit

Find every legacy cryptographic dependency: vaults and key managers, certificates, libraries, protocols, data at rest and in transit, and the third-party components that carry their own cryptography with them.

02

Evaluate readiness

Score each system for lattice-based readiness: algorithm support, key-size and performance impact, hybrid-mode options, and vendor posture. Readiness is measured per system, not asserted per enterprise.

03

Orchestrate the transition

Phase the migration under policy: hybrid deployments first, cutovers gated on verification, rollback paths kept live. Zero-trust transitions — no standing trust carried across the boundary unverified.

04

Prove every step

Every migration action lands in an evidence ledger: what was re-keyed, when, under which policy, verified how. Auditors and regulators get a record, not a narrative.

How the migration works

Five stages, in order. The sequence matters: exposure first, algorithms second, cutover last.

1

Inventory

Map all cryptographic assets and dependencies. You cannot migrate what you have not found, and most enterprises have not found it all.

2

Prioritize by secrecy lifetime

Data that must stay secret longest migrates first. Harvest-now-decrypt-later turns the migration order into a risk calculation, not a convenience order.

3

Pilot in hybrid

Run classical and post-quantum algorithms together on a bounded set of systems. Measure performance, compatibility, and failure modes before anything load-bearing moves.

4

Cut over under policy

Each cutover is gated: verification evidence required, rollback available, authority to proceed explicit. No silent transitions, no standing trust carried across unverified.

5

Verify continuously

Post-migration, the inventory keeps running. New code, new vendors, and new certificates are checked against the post-quantum policy as they arrive — the migration ends as a standing control, not a project.
